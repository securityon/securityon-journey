I attended the **Elsevier Author Workshop** in Seoul on 29 September. The session followed the process of preparing and submitting a journal manuscript from a publisher's perspective. In “Towards a Conference Publication”, I had written about developing my research into a paper. This workshop helped me connect that goal to the structure of a manuscript and the criteria an editor applies.

The session covered manuscript preparation, publication and peer review, research integrity, generative AI, and resources for researchers. I have selected the points that connect most closely to my own research plans. This account does not replace the current instructions of any particular journal.

## Start with the contribution

Before writing, I need to be able to say **what the work adds** and **who needs to read it**. The workshop emphasised a focused contribution supported by reliable evidence and a journal whose audience fits the work. Choosing a journal first and forcing a study into its scope would reverse that reasoning.

One practical suggestion was to arrange the main findings through figures and tables before refining the prose. A draft can then be revised from the reader's perspective. I took this as a way to test whether the evidence supports the central claim, rather than a way to make finished results look more attractive. When I prepare a manuscript, I want to write down the research question, main result and contribution before expanding the sections.

## Keep the same question in view throughout the paper

The title and abstract are the first parts readers and editors encounter. A useful title identifies the subject and actual finding; the abstract explains what was done and found without requiring the rest of the paper. Vague opening phrases, internal abbreviations and claims that exceed the data obscure that message.

The introduction establishes the problem and research gap. The methods give enough detail to understand and assess the approach. The results show what was observed, while the discussion interprets those observations in context and addresses their limits. Figures and tables should carry necessary evidence without repeating the same information in the text. A conclusion can then state the contribution and remaining questions without replaying the whole paper.

The workshop also stressed short, direct sentences, one idea at a time and consistent tense. For me, clear reasoning needs to come before elaborate English phrasing.

## How editors and reviewers read the work

The session described an editor's first reading as a check of the title and abstract for topic and journal fit, followed by the introduction for the research question and gap. If I cannot explain why the work belongs in a journal and matters to its readers, I should revisit the choice of venue.

A cover letter should do more than repeat the abstract. The advice was to keep it to one page, state the aim, main finding, novelty and journal fit, and include only context the editor needs, such as a special issue or earlier correspondence. At submission, I would still need to follow that journal's Guide for Authors for declarations and supporting material.

The workshop also showed how to respond to reviewers: address each point separately, identify the change and its location, and explain disagreement with evidence and a professional tone. I want to use that process to examine the argument and its support, rather than treat a revision as a formality.

## Authorship and disclosure

The ethics section covered plagiarism, distorted results, duplicate submission and undisclosed interests. Authorship needs to be settled before submission. The slides described an author as someone who makes a substantial contribution to the work, takes part in drafting or important revision, approves the final version and accepts responsibility for it. Language editing alone and honorary authorship were presented as different from that role.

Research support and relevant relationships also need to be disclosed. Because the exact declaration requirements belong to the target journal, it would help to keep a record of contributions, support and possible interests while the research is under way.

## Generative AI as assistance, with responsibility retained by the author

The workshop materials described possible uses of generative AI for literature exploration, organising ideas and polishing language. They also placed responsibility for accuracy, originality, data protection and intellectual property with the author, and discussed disclosure of AI use at submission.

The slides were more specific about images. They distinguished limited use for explanatory diagrams and visualisations derived from underlying data from restrictions on creating or altering primary research images, such as microscopy images, and on generating graphical abstracts with AI. Where AI is itself part of the research method, its use needs to be described in the methods. These were the points presented **at the September 2026 workshop**; I would check the target journal's latest guidance before submitting.

For reviewers and editors, the presentation emphasised manuscript confidentiality and independent human judgement. As an author, I also need to decide what research material can be entered into an external tool and keep track of what the tool assisted with and what I verified myself.

## What I will carry into the next manuscript

The workshop gave me a clearer order of work. First, I should state a contribution that the results support and test its logic against the figures, tables and draft. Then I should check the readership and submission requirements of a suitable journal. Finally, I should make authorship, competing interests and AI use clear in the manuscript and submission material.

My longer-term goal remains to develop the research towards a suitable conference or journal submission. This workshop dealt mainly with **journal publication**; a conference submission would require its own format and policy checks. I intend to use these questions when preparing a manuscript.
